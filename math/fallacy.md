# Fallacy

A **fallacy** is an error in reasoning that results in an
[[argument form#valid-and-invalid-arguments|invalid argument]]

Three common fallacies are :

1. using ambiguous premises
2. circular resoning
3. jumping to conclusion

## Converse error

A **converse error** is another fallacy. This fallacy take the following
argument form

$$
p \implies q
$$
$$
q
$$
$$
\therefore p
$$

This fallacy arise because the conclusion of the argument would follow from the
premises if the premises $p \implies q$ were replaced by its
[[conditional statement#converse-of-a-conditional-statement|converse]]

Such a replacement is not allowed because a conditional statement is not
[[logical equivalent|logicaly equivalent]] to its converse

This fallacy is also known as the **fallacy of affirming the consequent**

**example** : "If it rains then the street is wet. The street is wet. Therefore
it is raining." — wrong, the street could be wet from a hose

## Inverse Error

A **inverse error** is a fallacy. This fallacy take the following form

$$
p \implies q
$$
$$
\neg p
$$
$$
\therefore \neg q
$$

This fallacy arise because the conclusion of the argument would follow from the
premises if the premise $p \implies q$ were replaced by its
[[conditional statement#inverse-of-a-conditional-statement|inverse]]

Such a replacement is not allowed because a conditional statement is not
[[logical equivalent|logicaly equivalent]] to its inverse

This fallacy is also known as the **fallacy of denying the antecedent**

**example** : "If it rains then the street is wet. It is not raining. Therefore
the street is not wet." — wrong, the street could still be wet from a hose
